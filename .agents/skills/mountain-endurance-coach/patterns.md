# Patterns

## AeT Drift Test
**When to use**: When you need a field check of aerobic durability or want to see whether base training is improving economy at a stable effort.

**How**: Run or hike-run for $30$–$60$ minutes at a steady Z2 HR cap on comparable terrain. Compare output in the first half versus the second half and note HR drift, pace change, and perceived effort.

**Trade-offs**: Heat, dehydration, poor sleep, and terrain variability can distort the result. Use as a trend tool, not a verdict from heaven.

## AeT–LT Gap Interpretation
**When to use**: When deciding whether an athlete needs more base work or can profitably absorb more threshold training.

**How**: Determine AeT and LT from lab or field data, compute the percentage gap, and route the athlete toward base-heavy, base-plus-strides, or threshold-ready training.

**Trade-offs**: Threshold estimates are only as good as the tests behind them. Do not turn a fuzzy estimate into a rigid identity.

## Base-Building Week
**When to use**: Early in a cycle, after detraining, or whenever durability lags behind ambition.

**How**: Keep most work in Z1–Z2, distribute one long outing, one medium-long outing, one or two recovery sessions, and one or two strength sessions. Progress total time more conservatively than ego prefers.

**Trade-offs**: Feels unglamorous. The athlete must buy into repeatability over excitement.

## 3:1 Load Cycle
**When to use**: For athletes who absorb load best with regular unloading and who drift toward overdoing things when left unsupervised.

**How**: Accumulate three progressive weeks, then cut total load in week four while preserving movement frequency and a small amount of rhythm work.

**Trade-offs**: Not universal. Some athletes prefer 2:1 or longer mesocycles if life stress is low and training history is deep.

## Key-Workout Sandwich
**When to use**: To protect a key session from both stale legs and poor follow-up recovery.

**How**: Put the key workout between an easier lead-in day and an easier or rest day after it. Use this for threshold sessions, long runs, downhill exposure, and race simulations.

**Trade-offs**: Requires calendar discipline. Social runs and extra junk volume tend to ruin the sandwich.

## General Strength Stage Ladder
**When to use**: When building from weak or unstable basics toward more demanding running-specific strength.

**How**: Progress from basic movement quality and unilateral control to more demanding leg, hip, upper-body, and trunk patterns. Advance only when the current stage is technically stable.

**Trade-offs**: Slow by design. Skipping ahead often saves time only until it produces an injury detour.

## Max Strength Microblock
**When to use**: In early-to-mid base when the athlete has enough bandwidth to handle heavy work without compromising key endurance sessions.

**How**: Use low reps, high quality, long rest, and controlled exercise selection for $2$ sessions per week before reducing to maintenance.

**Trade-offs**: High neuromuscular cost. Bad timing turns strength work into a hidden fatigue bomb.

## Muscular-Endurance Uphill Set
**When to use**: When preparing for long climbs, steep hiking, or events where force must stay repeatable deep into fatigue.

**How**: Use sustained uphill work, steep treadmill bouts, or weighted circuits that mimic long climbing demands. Keep the intent specific rather than randomly heroic.

**Trade-offs**: ME is expensive and should not appear too early or coexist carelessly with heavy run intensity.

## Four-Discipline Race Audit
**When to use**: When a trail athlete performs well in one terrain type and unravels in another.

**How**: Grade the event and the athlete separately on climbing, runnable terrain, technical terrain, and descending. Train the missing discipline instead of doubling down on current strengths.

**Trade-offs**: Requires honest assessment. Athletes often overidentify with their best skill and neglect the rest.

## Downhill Skill Session
**When to use**: Before technical or descent-heavy races, or when quad soreness and hesitation erase late-race performance.

**How**: Use short technical descents with high focus on vision, line choice, cadence, posture, and foot placement. Add controlled eccentric exposure, not chaos.

**Trade-offs**: Too much too soon creates soreness and injury risk. Skill comes from frequent touch, not one death-march descent.

## Heat Acclimation Block
**When to use**: Before hot races or training camps when the climate gap is too large to ignore.

**How**: Build repeated heat exposure through easy runs in heat, sauna, overdressed indoor sessions, or postrun heat work. Keep the intensity low while the body adapts.

**Trade-offs**: Heat adds load. Athletes often confuse acclimation with fitness and dig themselves into a hole.

## Gut Training Progression
**When to use**: Before races where fueling failure is a major risk or carbohydrate intake needs to rise.

**How**: Practice race-like fluids and carbohydrates in long runs and key sessions. Start with tolerable doses, then progress concentration, frequency, and total intake.

**Trade-offs**: GI adaptation lags behind enthusiasm. Novel products and big jumps are usually punished.

## Failure-Point Premortem
**When to use**: Two to four weeks before a goal race, or when an athlete repeatedly melts down in familiar ways.

**How**: Ask what is most likely to fail first: pacing, heat, GI, descending, cramping, sleepiness, aid-station chaos, low mood, or terrain mismatch. Build one prevention tactic and one rescue tactic for each.

**Trade-offs**: Works only if the athlete is honest about likely weaknesses instead of rehearsing a fantasy highlight reel.

## Process-Goal Stack
**When to use**: When pressure rises, outcomes dominate attention, or the athlete races worse than they train.

**How**: Build a short chain of controllables such as HR cap, fueling clock, posture cue, breathing reset, and climb-to-hike switch point.

**Trade-offs**: Too many process goals become clutter. Keep only the few that reliably improve decisions.

## Female Athlete Adjustment Loop
**When to use**: When symptoms, cycle phase, perimenopausal changes, GI tolerance, or recovery patterns are affecting training quality.

**How**: Track symptoms, recovery, fueling tolerance, and session response alongside training load. Adjust intensity, fueling, or recovery before forcing compliance with the plan.

**Trade-offs**: Requires consistent logging and pattern recognition; shortcuts usually default back to the wrong baseline.

## Post-Race Recovery Block
**When to use**: After a goal race, training camp, or any block that produced unusual musculoskeletal or nervous-system fatigue.

**How**: Restore movement first, structure second, intensity last. Keep frequency with very easy work, assess soreness and motivation, then rebuild gradually.

**Trade-offs**: Ambitious athletes often cut this phase short and spend weeks pretending they are “back” while training like cardboard.
