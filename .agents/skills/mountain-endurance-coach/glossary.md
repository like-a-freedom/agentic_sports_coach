# Glossary

**A race** — The season’s highest-priority event; training peaks and taper timing should serve this target. (ch04, ch12)

**AeT (Aerobic Threshold)** — The highest intensity at which aerobic metabolism still dominates and the athlete can usually accumulate large amounts of work without disproportionate fatigue. Often used as the Z2 HR cap. (ch02)

**AeT–LT gap** — The percentage difference between aerobic and lactate thresholds. Large gaps suggest a stronger need for base development before more intensity. (ch02)

**aerobic base** — The accumulated low-intensity training capacity that improves mitochondrial density, capillarization, fuel flexibility, and repeatability. (ch01, ch03)

**aerobic decoupling / HR drift** — A growing separation between power or pace and heart rate over time at a constant effort; often used as a field signal of aerobic durability or fatigue. (ch02, ch07)

**altitude acclimation** — The process of adapting to reduced oxygen availability through staged exposure, pacing restraint, and recovery management. (ch08)

**anaerobic glycolysis** — Fast ATP production from carbohydrate when energy demand rises beyond what aerobic pathways can supply alone. (ch01)

**ATP-PCr system** — The immediate high-power energy system that fuels very short, explosive efforts. (ch01)

**ATL (Acute Training Load)** — A short-term view of recent training stress that helps approximate fatigue. (ch07)

**B race** — An important event used for practice, feedback, or partial peaking, but not at the expense of the A race. (ch04)

**base period** — The phase focused on building aerobic capacity, durability, and movement economy before more race-specific intensity. (ch03)

**capacity + opportunity + motivation** — Jornet’s planning lens: what the athlete can do, what the context allows, and what the athlete is willing and able to sustain. (ch04, ch11)

**central governor theory** — The idea that the brain helps regulate output to protect the organism from catastrophic failure, shaping pacing and perceived effort. (ch10)

**circadian stability** — Consistent sleep, wake, meal, and training rhythms that improve recovery and reduce avoidable stress. (ch04, ch07)

**CTL (Chronic Training Load)** — A longer-term view of accumulated training stress that helps approximate fitness or load tolerance. (ch07)

**decoupling** — A practical synonym for aerobic drift; worsening separation between output and physiological response over time. (ch02, ch07)

**deload / recovery week** — A planned reduction in load that preserves frequency while allowing fatigue to dissipate and adaptations to consolidate. (ch03, ch07)

**Demands of the Event (DoE)** — A framework that maps external course demands to internal system stress so training can target the real bottlenecks. (ch12)

**DOMS** — Delayed onset muscle soreness; a sign of tissue stress that should be interpreted in context rather than automatically feared. (ch07)

**downhill specificity** — Training directed at eccentric tolerance, vision, foot placement, and technical decision-making for descents. (ch06)

**economy / movement economy** — The energy cost of moving at a given speed or grade. Better economy means spending less energy for the same output. (ch01)

**energy availability** — The energy left for basic physiology after training expenditure is accounted for. Low availability raises RED-S risk. (ch11)

**failure point** — The system or constraint most likely to break performance first: GI, heat, glycogen, neuromuscular fatigue, decision quality, and so on. (ch12)

**fatigue matrix** — A practical distinction between muscular fatigue and energetic fatigue, useful for choosing recovery or adjusting training. (ch07)

**four disciplines** — Koop’s split of trail performance into climbing, flat/runnable terrain, technical terrain, and descending. (ch06)

**general strength** — Foundational strength work for posture, joint control, basic movement competence, and durability. (ch05)

**glycogen** — Stored carbohydrate in muscle and liver; a limited but powerful fuel source for harder exercise. (ch01, ch09)

**gut training** — Progressive practice of race fueling so absorption, comfort, and decision confidence improve before competition. (ch09)

**heat acclimation** — Repeated heat exposure that improves thermoregulation, sweating, and comfort in hot conditions. (ch08)

**HR drift** — See aerobic decoupling. (ch02, ch07)

**individuality principle** — The rule that similar training inputs can produce different responses in different athletes, so plans must be adjusted to context. (ch03, ch11)

**lactate shuttle** — The process by which lactate is transported and reused as fuel instead of treated as useless waste. (ch01)

**lactate threshold (LT)** — The intensity where lactate production outpaces clearance enough to mark the upper end of sustainable steady work. (ch01, ch02)

**macrocycle** — The season-level training arc organizing phases toward key races. (ch03, ch04)

**mesocycle** — A multiweek block inside a macrocycle, often devoted to a single emphasis such as base, hill strength, or heat preparation. (ch03, ch04)

**microcycle** — The short planning unit, usually a week, that distributes key sessions and recovery. (ch04)

**movement economy** — See economy. (ch01)

**muscular endurance (ME)** — The ability to repeat submaximal force under fatigue, especially important for long climbs and sustained trail demands. (ch05)

**NGP (Normalized Graded Pace)** — A grade-adjusted pace metric used to compare effort across variable terrain. (ch02)

**overload and recovery** — The paired principle that training must be challenging enough to stimulate adaptation and easy enough to absorb it. (ch03)

**power hiking** — Fast uphill hiking used on grades where running becomes inefficient or overly costly. It is a trainable skill. (ch06)

**progression** — Planned increases in difficulty, density, duration, vertical, or specificity over time. (ch03)

**RPE (Rating of Perceived Exertion)** — The athlete’s subjective intensity scale; useful for cross-checking HR, pace, and terrain. (ch02, ch10)

**RED-S** — Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport; a syndrome caused by chronically low energy availability that can impair health and performance. (ch11)

**recovery modalities** — Tools that support restoration, such as sleep, nutrition, light movement, mobility, compression, cooling, or heat exposure. (ch07)

**specific period** — The phase where training most closely resembles the event’s terrain, fueling, pacing, and fatigue profile. (ch03)

**specificity** — The principle that training should reflect the demands of the event you want to perform in. (ch03, ch06)

**taper** — The final reduction in load before a key event, intended to preserve sharpness while shedding fatigue. (ch03, ch12)

**technicality** — The coordination, balance, line choice, and risk-management demands imposed by terrain complexity. (ch06)

**thermoregulation** — The body’s effort to maintain safe core temperature under heat, cold, humidity, wind, and solar stress. (ch08)

**TSB (Training Stress Balance)** — A simple fit-versus-fatigued view derived from chronic and acute load trends. (ch07)

**vertical gain budget** — The weekly or block-level amount of climbing and descending an athlete can absorb productively. (ch04, ch06)

**VO2max** — Maximum aerobic capacity; the highest rate at which the body can take up and use oxygen. (ch01)

**Z1–Z5** — Intensity bands anchored by threshold and perception, ranging from easy recovery to maximal efforts. (ch02)
